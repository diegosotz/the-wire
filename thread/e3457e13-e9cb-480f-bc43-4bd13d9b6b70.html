Federal agencies release 2025 Shared National Credit Program report amid private credit stress signals

Federal banking agencies released the 2025 Shared National Credit Program report January 12, providing updated guidance on syndicated lending risk assessment frameworks. The SNC program examines credit exposures of $100 million or more shared by multiple supervised institutions, covering approximately $5.2 trillion in commitments as of the 2024 review cycle.

The timing coincides with ongoing private credit market stress signals, as the SNC framework directly overlaps with leveraged lending markets where private credit competes with traditional bank syndications. Updated guidance typically influences how banks assess credit risk in leveraged transactions, potentially affecting capital allocation decisions between traditional syndicated lending and private credit partnerships.

The report's release follows a pattern of regulatory attention to credit market conditions, with agencies historically using SNC updates to signal supervisory priorities during periods of market stress. Previous SNC guidance shifts have preceded broader changes in bank lending standards, particularly in leveraged finance markets where private credit firms source deal flow.

Assessment

The SNC report release suggests regulatory awareness of current credit market conditions, with timing indicating potential supervisory concern about leveraged lending quality amid private credit sector stress. If the guidance tightens risk assessment standards, banks may further reduce exposure to private credit partnerships, accelerating the institutional deleveraging already observed at JPMorgan. Conversely, unchanged guidance would signal regulatory comfort with current credit conditions, potentially contradicting market stress signals.

Watch For

  • SNC report content for any tightening of leveraged lending risk assessment criteria
  • Bank earnings calls in coming weeks for commentary on SNC guidance impact on private credit partnerships

Oaktree's Marks dismisses systemic private credit risk, cites expansion pace as only concern

Oaktree Capital's Howard Marks stated March 5 that private credit markets face no systemic problems, countering recent stress signals from institutional lenders and retail market reactions. Marks acknowledged risk only from the "pace of expansion in direct lending," referencing the sector's growth from inception around 2011 to over $1 trillion currently.

The comments come one day before Robinhood's venture fund debut decline and amid JPMorgan's lending reduction to private credit firms following software loan markdowns. Marks' assessment directly contradicts market behavior suggesting broader private market reassessment across investor categories.

Oaktree manages approximately $190 billion in alternative investments, making Marks' perspective significant for institutional sentiment. However, his dismissal of systemic risk conflicts with observable deleveraging patterns from major banks and retail investor rejection of private market exposure through public vehicles.

Assessment

Marks' comments represent institutional pushback against private credit stress narratives, but timing suggests defensive positioning rather than genuine confidence. His acknowledgment of expansion pace risk validates concerns about market structure while minimizing broader implications. The disconnect between his assessment and concurrent market behavior (JPMorgan deleveraging, retail rejection) indicates either information asymmetry or strategic messaging to prevent further institutional flight from the sector.

Robinhood's venture fund debuts down 11%, signaling retail investor retreat from private markets

Robinhood's Venture Fund I dropped 11% on its March 6 NYSE debut, marking a sharp rejection by public market investors of exposure to private company assets. The fund, designed to give retail investors access to pre-IPO companies, closed its first trading session significantly below its initial pricing. JPMorgan reduces lending to private credit firms following software loan markdowns

JPMorgan Chase has curtailed lending to private credit firms after marking down a portfolio of software company loans, according to CNBC reporting March 11. The nation's largest bank by assets has tightened credit availability to alternative asset managers amid deteriorating performance in technology sector exposures. The move affects capital flows to private credit funds that rely on bank financing for leverage and liquidity management.

Private credit markets have expanded to approximately $1.7 trillion in assets under management as of Q4 2025, with software and technology companies representing roughly 30% of deal flow according to Preqin data. JPMorgan's credit pullback comes as software valuations face pressure from higher discount rates and slower growth trajectories. The bank's lending reduction constrains funding sources for private credit managers at a time when portfolio companies may require additional capital support.

The timing coincides with broader signs of stress in alternative credit markets. Private credit default rates reached 4.2% in Q4 2025, up from 2.1% a year prior, while deal completion rates declined 23% year-over-year. JPMorgan's action suggests major banks are reassessing counterparty risk in the private credit ecosystem as underlying asset quality deteriorates.

Assessment

JPMorgan's move signals potential systemic tightening in private credit funding mechanisms. The bank's dual role as both a lender to private credit firms and a holder of similar assets creates information advantages in risk assessment. When the largest U.S. bank reduces exposure, it typically reflects internal stress testing that anticipates broader market deterioration. The software sector focus is particularly concerning given its outsized representation in private credit portfolios and sensitivity to interest rate environments. This appears to be early-stage deleveraging rather than routine credit cycle management.

  • Other major banks (Bank of America, Wells Fargo, Citi) announcing similar private credit exposure reductions
